ABOUT THIS EPISODE

PREVIEW — IN ENGLISH

Thursday began with a car that was not a truck.

It sat at the back corner of the lot, near the service entrance, where in six years Rita had seen delivery vans and Ellis's van and once a state trooper with a flat — and never a clean gray sedan with Jackson plates, parked square and early, before the room's own day had started. A car that arrives before five-thirty is not passing through. It has come for the building.

Rita stood a moment in the back doorway with her keys in her hand and looked at it, the way she looked at weather she had not invited. Then she went in and did the day in its order. Lights. Coffee on. Stools down, last stool first. Pie case: two chess, one coconut, half a chocolate. At the pass she cut Thursday's slice of chess and covered it and set it on the back shelf, and her knife did not stop over the pie. Whatever the car had come for, the day had not asked her to stop feeding people, and habit — which had won every week of this hard month — won this one before the room was even lit. She put her name tag on over her heart and counted the drawer and opened the room, and the printed notice hung on the front door glass and she did not read it. The paper that mattered today was not on the door. It was still out in the lot, in a gray car, waiting for office hours.

The Thursday cast came in on their clocks, and the room, for all its papers and all its visitors, was still the room.

The produce man came in at a quarter past six with his cap cold from the lot, Thursday his double day, and took his counter stool like a scholar well settled into his studies.

"Heard a car out back," he said. "Sedan. Not a truck in the world sits that low."

"You heard right," Rita said.

"Man don't come before daylight to eat. He comes before daylight to count something."

"Then he'll find us countable," Rita said, and set his check where he could reach it, because on his double day he paid standing up.

The tanker man out of Memphis took the last stool and turned his cup once in its saucer and said that if there was counting to be done it had better include the coffee, because the coffee was the load-bearing wall of the whole establishment, and Rita fed him like a man whose engineering was sound. Old Harlan came in at seven with his newspaper and one opinion already unfolded, and for once the opinion had a date in it.

"Today's the day, Rita. Man at the fuel desk told me straight this morning. Equipment man. Every old kitchen on the corridor list gets looked at in person before anything's signed anywhere. We're to be verified." He said the word the way a man says a word given him by somebody with a clipboard. "First they measured. Then they posted. Now they come and look at the box itself."

"A man can see a sign," Rita said, and refilled him without being asked. "What comes today, we'll see today. Eat your eggs while they're a rumor and not a report."

Harlan allowed that eggs were best eaten ahead of all reports whatsoever, and turned to the sports page, and the morning went on — whole, and ordinary, and lit.

The auditor came in through the front door at twenty past one, in the lull after the lunch hour, with a clipboard under his arm and a gray case in his hand.
