PREVIEW — IN ENGLISH

Thursday came around again, the way Thursdays did, whether anybody was ready for what was in them or not.

Rita found the room at five-thirty in its order. Lights. Coffee on. Stools down, last stool first. Pie case: two chess, one coconut, half a chocolate. The printed notice was still on the inside of the front door glass, straight and squared where the evening crew had taped it, and this morning she did not read it.

She had read it twice the first morning and once a day since, the way you read a thing you are memorizing against your will. Today she went up front to unlock with the paper in the corner of her eye and left it there, unread, the way you leave a scar. The date on it was a week nearer than it had been. Reading it again would not move the date and would not move her, and Thursday does not wait for a woman to stand at a door.

At the pass she took down a plate and a square of wrap and stood with the chess pie in front of her and her knife in her hand, and stopped.

It was not a decision she was making. It was a thing her hands had done on Thursdays for a year and more, ahead of the rest of her — cover a slice, set it on the back shelf, where it would wait the whole day for twenty to ten. Last Thursday she had done it before the rest of her had caught up. Last Thursday night she had taken the wrap off it in a kitchen where the words underneath everything had changed, and he had eaten it standing, and washed the plate, and gone.

Her knife stayed where it was, over the pie, a moment longer than a cut takes.

Then she cut the slice and covered it and set it on the back shelf, because the day had not asked her to stop feeding people and she was not going to be the one who taught it how. What the slice meant was a question for later, if there was a later. What it was, was chess pie, on a plate, covered, in its place.

She counted the drawer and put her name tag on over her heart. RITA. Hair pinned up, pencil behind one ear. The hand-lettered sign on the register said what it had said since last year — KITCHEN CLOSES 9:30 — and beside it now, in her head if not on the glass, there were two dates in the room where there had been one, and both of them were coming.

The Thursday cast came in on their clocks, the way they always came, and the notice had changed its nature in a week, the way things did in that room. It was not news anymore. It was weather with a date on it, and the cast read it the way they read weather — for what it would do to their own road.

The produce man came in at a quarter past six with his cap cold from the lot, Thursday his double day, and he ate his breakfast at the counter.

Not in his booth. At the counter, on a stool, with his plate in front of him and his elbows in the narrow space a stool allows a man, and Rita understood standing there with the coffee pot that he was rehearsing. He was living his after-six supper a month early, at breakfast, to see how it fit.

"How's it sit?" she said.

"Like a man eating at a fence," he said. "A man can do it. Men do everything. That's not the same as it being supper." He ate the whole plate there, without hurry and without pleasure either, a man paying a debt in advance. When he stood up he squared his cap and looked once at his booth — second one down, where he had eaten on Thursdays since before she had the counter — and then he looked away from it the way you look away from a house you used to live in, passing. "First of the month," he said, "I'll already know how. That's something."
