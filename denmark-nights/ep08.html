PREVIEW — IN ENGLISH

Saturday started with the book.

Rita came in at five-thirty through the back, the way she had come in for six years, and she did the day in its order — lights, coffee on, stools down, last stool first, pie case counted: two chess, one coconut, half a chocolate. The hand-lettered sign on the register said what it had said since last year — KITCHEN CLOSES 9:30 — and the printed notice on the inside of the front door glass hung where it had hung all week, straight and squared, and she did not read it. A week of not reading it on Thursdays had taught her hands the habit on Saturdays too.

Then, before the first trucker of the morning could come through the door wanting the day started, she took the worn notebook down from behind the register, shook the rubber band off it, and read Friday's page.

Fridays filled pages. She had closed this one herself at ten the night before, tired enough that her small square hand had gone smaller still toward the bottom, and Saturday morning was when she read what Friday had actually left behind — because a thing written down at midnight and a thing understood at dawn were not always the same thing, and the road's word deserved a keeper who read it twice.

A phone number for a driver due through Sunday, left by his brother. A couch in Jackson, offered again and still unclaimed. A name signed under another name, a debt forgiven in a margin. All of it ordinary. All of it somebody's whole errand, kept in a book behind a register, waiting for the road to come back around and ask for it.

Rita closed the book on Friday and put the rubber band around it twice and set it back where it lived, and picked up the coffee pot, because Saturday does not let a counter woman stand still either — it just sends her a different crowd to prove it with.

Saturday's crowd was travelers. Rita had learned it the way she learned everything in that room, by the cups. Weekdays belonged to the road's working people, who came in on clocks and ate like it. Saturdays belonged to people the road had borrowed — families in cars loaded to the windows, kids already tired at breakfast and asking what state they were in. They read the menu like a document. They asked where the bathrooms were. Rita fed them all the same, and if she was honest — and she was, at five-thirty in the morning, before the day had given her any reason not to be — she liked Saturdays. The room on a Saturday was the room being what it was for people who would only ever see it once.

Dee came on at two with her school envelope in her apron, took one look at the pie case, and said the case wanted a chess pie by supper or there would be words, and Rita said words were free and pie was not and to keep her apron on. The afternoon ran in its traveler waves. A woman driving alone to Memphis asked Rita, in all seriousness, whether the road got any better past Jackson, and Rita told her the road got the same and the company got better, which the woman appeared to take as an answer.

At twenty minutes to five, the day changed its mind about what kind of Saturday it was going to be.

It came in the way trouble mostly came in off that exit — not fast, and not loud. A minivan rolled down the frontage past the fuel islands with its hazard lights blinking and no engine sound under it at all, coasting on whatever the ramp had given it, and it used the last of that gift to turn, slow as a held breath, into the car side of the lot, where it stopped at an angle across two spaces and sat there ticking in the heat.

Rita watched it through the front glass. A man got out and stood looking at the hood the way men look at hoods, as if the looking were a tool. A woman opened the sliding door, and two children unfolded themselves out of the back seat the way children do after hours in a car — all at once and in pieces, a boy of seven holding a shoe, a girl of four holding nothing, having decided that holding things was over.
