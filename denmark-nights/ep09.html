PREVIEW — IN ENGLISH

Thursday began with a count.

Rita came in at five-thirty through the back, the way she had come in for six years, and she did the day in its order, only today the order ran a step ahead of her hands. Lights. Coffee on. Stools down, last stool first. Pie case: two chess, one coconut, half a chocolate — the same count the case had closed on, and under it, already waiting in her head before the room was even lit, tonight's work: the shells. Friday's case would not fill itself, and cream pies could not be hurried in the morning — a coconut or a chocolate shell had to be baked clean through the night before and stand empty and cool for its filling, and the filling itself needed its hours in the cold to set. So on Thursday nights the kitchen's last oven run before nine-thirty was shells, and after close, when the room was hers again, the shells were Rita's to count and put away. She took a slip of paper at the pass and wrote the list down instead of trusting her head with it: coconut, six, baked through. Chocolate, six, baked through. Chess, six, lined raw, to take their filling in the morning and go into the oven with it. She set the slip under the ticket spike where she would see it all day.

The printed notice hung on the inside of the front door glass, straight and squared, and she did not read it. Her hands had learned that the way they had learned everything this month.

At the pass she took down a plate and a square of wrap and cut Thursday's slice of chess and covered it and set it on the back shelf. Last week her knife had stopped over that pie a moment longer than a cut takes. This morning it did not stop. Habit had won the week, and she let it have the win without a speech, and put her name tag on over her heart, and counted the drawer, and opened the room.

The Thursday cast came in on their clocks, and the room, for all its papers, was still the room — whole, and lit, and with laughter left in it.

The produce man came in at a quarter past six with his cap cold from the lot, Thursday his double day, and took the counter stool he had been rehearsing on last week, and ate his breakfast there like a man paying a small bill early so it would not be waiting for him later.

"Second week of school," he said, "and I near sat in the booth out of habit. Muscle's got a longer memory than the man attached to it."

"You sat down in the right place," Rita said. "That's the whole of the lesson. The rest is just eating."

"Then I'm a scholar," he said, and ate like one, and paid standing up, the way he paid on his double day, and went out to his trailer with the day in front of him.

The tanker man out of Memphis took the last stool without looking at it and turned his cup once in its saucer and announced, to the room generally, that he had read no papers and signed nothing and intended to keep it that way until supper at least, and Rita fed him like a man whose intentions were sound. Old Harlan came in at seven with his newspaper and one opinion already unfolded ahead of the paper, and delivered it to his eggs — a fellow at the Jackson store had told a fellow that the corridor list had a date on it now, names and all, and wasn't that the shape of things — and Rita refilled him without being asked and told him a fellow telling a fellow was still not a sign he could read off a wall, and Harlan allowed that it was early yet for wall-reading and turned to the sports page with the air of a man whose point had been made someplace, even if not here.

The flower woman's booth sat empty. Her Thursday was every other Thursday, and this was the other one, and Rita noted the gap the way she noted weather — recorded, and made nothing of.
