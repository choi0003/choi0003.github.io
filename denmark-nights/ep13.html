PREVIEW — IN ENGLISH

The first Thursday with the quiet box began under the coffee.

Rita came in at five-thirty through the back, the way she had come in for six years, and she did the day in its order. Lights. Coffee on. Stools down, last stool first. But underneath the morning's ordinary sounds the walk-in's new note held, lower and even and unhurried, a box keeping its cold without working for it, and she stood at the pass a moment and listened to her own kitchen for the one sound that had changed.

It had been a week of it. A week of mornings where the note did not climb. Noticing was her trade, and she noticed every morning.

Pie case: one chess, one coconut, half a chocolate. At the pass she cut Thursday's slice of chess and covered it and set it on the back shelf, where it had waited every Thursday of the season, through worse numbers than these. The dial over the walk-in door said thirty-six, and holding. She wrote the morning line in the temperature log, in her small square hand, under the line in the square hand that was not hers, and put her name tag on over her heart, and counted the drawer, and opened the room at six.

Somewhere between Jackson and this counter a piece of paper was still travelling — the countersign that would finish what the verification had started. Paper moved at the speed of paper. Rita did not stand watching for it. Thursday did not wait for paper, and neither did she.

The Thursday cast came in on their clocks, and the room, with its quieter box and its travelling paper, was still the room.

The produce man came in at a quarter past six with his cap cold from the lot. Thursday was his double day — Memphis in the morning empty and back through by dark full — and he took his counter stool and said, to the room generally, "Box sounds like it quit arguing."

"Box sounds like it's doing its work without telling everybody," Rita said, and put the big breakfast in front of him before he had finished deciding to order it, and the coffee black, and the check where he could reach it, because on his double day he paid standing up and hated to wait for arithmetic.

The tanker man out of Memphis took the last stool without looking at it. Thursday was his far point — he turned around tonight and slept in his own bed tomorrow — and tonight his company had moved the turn late, so he would roll at ten instead of eight, which meant, turning his cup once in its saucer, that a man got two extra hours of Thursday and had best spend them somewhere with coffee in it.

Old Harlan came in at seven with his newspaper and his opinions and wanted his eggs drowned, and Thursday was Harlan's long day at the counter, nowhere on earth he had to be. He had heard about the box the way Harlan heard about everything, thoroughly and from two directions.

"Man at the fuel desk says your box passed its examination," Harlan announced, over the eggs. "Says there's paper coming to bury the paper that was going to bury it."

"Paper'll come when it comes, Harlan," Rita said, and refilled him without being asked. "Eat your eggs while they're ahead of all the stamps." And Harlan allowed the sports page to console him.

The woman driver who came through on Thursdays only, hauling flowers to Memphis, ate her chess pie for breakfast at the counter, unashamed, the road owing her one sweet thing a week and her collecting it. "One of these weeks I'll have the eggs like a citizen," she said.

"One of these weeks," Rita said, and the morning went on, whole and ordinary and lit, over a box that hummed lower than it had hummed in a year of Thursdays, and the room did not make a ceremony of it. The room's ceremonies were coffee and pie and a check laid where a man could reach it, and those it kept.
