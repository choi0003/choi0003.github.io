ABOUT THIS EPISODE

금요일의 카운터는 도로가 말을 남기는 자리다 — 장례 기금이 모이고, 차가 고장 난 운전자에게 빈 좌석이 이어지고, 전언이 오간다. Rita는 그 모든 것을 낡은 카운터 북 한 권과 자기 기억으로 맞추고, 금요일마다 옆자리에 신문을 접어 두는 남자의 자리를 지킨다. 그날 책에는 Rita의 글씨가 아닌, “— M.”이라고만 서명된 낯선 항목이 하나 남는다.

PREVIEW — IN ENGLISH

Friday announced itself before the lights did.

Rita came in at five-thirty with her keys already in her hand, through the back, the way she had come in for six years — but on Fridays she did one thing out of order, and she did it without thinking about it, the way a woman checks a child's forehead in passing. Before the coffee, before the stools, she took the worn notebook down from behind the register, shook the rubber band off it, and opened it flat on the counter to the last written page.

Fridays were the day the road left the most word. Weekend runs were being set or begged for. Men were turning toward home or away from it. Somebody's truck was always in a shop with its driver standing in this room holding a problem, and somebody else's empty seat was always rolling through at supper, and the distance between those two facts was Rita's Friday. She read the last page the way other people read the weather.

Lights. Coffee on. Stools down, last stool first. Pie case: two chess, one coconut, half a chocolate. The sign on the register said what it had said since last year — KITCHEN CLOSES 9:30 — and the photograph taped beside it, Merle's granddaughter in her pink hat, two months old and famous in this room, had curled at one corner. Rita straightened the tape, counted the drawer, and put her name tag on over her heart. RITA. Hair pinned up, pencil behind one ear.

Then she did the filing she had not done Thursday night, because some paper waited better in daylight.

Thursday's copy lay in her apron pocket. A tear slip, square capitals at the top — WKLY CK — and below it the date, and the two numbers read off the gauges in the dark, standing with a hand near the discharge line, listening to the note under the note.

56. 227.

Thursday before last it had been 58 and 241, doing the same work. Sunday, with the new fan doing its whole job, it had come down to 55 and 224. Now it sat at 56 and 227 — a hair back up, the way a tired thing breathed harder on a warm week, and no more than that. She knew what it meant, because it had been told to her in advance, the way everything about that box was told to her: plain, and before it could surprise her. That was the drift. A hair at a time. It was not trouble. It was the truth, told early, by numbers.

She slid the slip into the notebook in its place, in among the road's messages, behind last week's copy and the first one and all the rest of them, six years of paper in his hand. Her pencil wrote Thurs. under the corner in her small square hand, the way it always wrote it, in case memory ever needed help.

Her pencil stopped there.

Behind Thursday's slip there was the place where Sunday belonged. Sunday had been a whole day of work — a motor carried in a rag, a premium call turned down without the money ever being named, a building brought back into tune — and a thing that was not a call left no paper, and the book kept the shape of it the only way it could: as a blank, in order, between Thursday and Thursday.

Rita looked at the blank a moment. Then she turned the page to Friday, which was already waiting to be filled, and picked up the coffee pot, because Friday does not let a counter woman stand still with a pencil in her hand.
