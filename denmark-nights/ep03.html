PREVIEW — IN ENGLISH

Rita came in at five-thirty, the way she had come in for six years, through the back, with her keys already in her hand, and Sunday came in with her, quieter than the other days.

Sundays had their own weather at the stop. The lot slept late. Half the trucks in the rows sat dark with their curtains drawn and their drivers making up the sleep the week owed them, and the morning trade came in ones and twos instead of a rush — a driver with nowhere to be until Monday, a night man blinking at the light. Church would let out up the road toward noon and bring the families in their good clothes for Sunday dinner, and between now and then the room was hers at an easier pace, and Rita moved through the opening the way she moved through every opening, in the same order, so that if anything was wrong she would know it by the time she reached it.

Lights. Coffee on. Stools down off the counter, in reverse.

The walk-in was last on the round, the way it was always last. The dial over the door said thirty-eight. Holding. She stood a moment with her palm flat against the cold steel, the way she had stood in the spring, and then she went on, because thirty-eight was not the number today was about.

Today was about a fan.

Thursday night, gauges in his hand, coiling his hoses in their order, Ellis had said it the way he said everything about that box, without hurry and without decoration. The fan would be the first to go. Not this month, most likely. But a bearing did not ask a calendar's permission, and when it went it would go on a hot afternoon with a full box, and so he would come Sunday and change the motor out on his own time, before the box had to ask him in front of witnesses.

"Sunday's your day off," Rita had said.

"Sunday's quiet," Ellis had said. "A man can hear himself work."

So on Saturday night, at close, Rita had done a thing she did not examine, the way she had checked the back light on the first Thursday and would have denied the reason for under oath. She had rotated Sunday's stock forward with her own hands — the lunch meats and the opened cream and the pie fillings out of the walk-in and into the reach-in under the pass, the eggs racked by the grill, the Sunday chicken already portioned on its trays where the cook could reach it without once opening the big box. Ellis would have the walk-in's door standing open and shut all morning and its condenser dark for the length of a motor swap, and the room would not ask the box for anything until he was done. The lunch trade would run one-handed around his work, and nobody in a good shirt would ever know there had been a dance to arrange.

She had not told the cook why. There was nothing to tell. It was just how the room was set this morning, the way the light over the back door was just on.

The breakfast cook came in at five-fifty and looked at the reach-in, full past its usual full, and at the trays laid out in their order, and tied his apron.

"You expecting a war?" he said.

"Sunday," Rita said. "Box man's coming to change a fan before it changes itself."

"On a Sunday."

"On a Sunday," she said, and poured his coffee, and that was the whole of what she had to say about it, to him or to herself.

The morning went the slow way Sundays went. A driver asleep in the corner booth with his cap pulled down and his coffee going cold in front of him, left alone on purpose — a man asleep in a booth on a Sunday had paid for that sleep with a week, and the room let him collect it. Two team drivers split a plate of eggs and argued, kindly and without heat, about a brother-in-law. Rita kept the cups full and the room at its simmer and listened, the way she always listened, to the building's chord — and there it was, underneath the reach-in and the ice machine, out back where the condensing unit sat on its pad: the fan's note, with the roughness in it she had first heard in the spring, only coarser now. A catch in every turn. A wheel going over the same stone once a second, patient as a clock.
