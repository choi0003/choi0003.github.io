ABOUT THIS EPISODE

답을 내야 하는 달의 마지막 목요일, Ellis는 잭슨에서 승진을 수락하고 그 아래 놓인 폐기 명령들에 서명한다 — 덴마크가 첫 번째다. 그날 밤 그는 덴마크 출구를 속도를 줄이지 않고 지나치고, Rita는 그의 빈자리 곁에서 저녁 근무를 마친다. 그녀는 지난 목요일의 점검 사본을 카운터 북에서 꺼내 쓰레기통에 넣었다가, 다시 꺼낸다.

PREVIEW — IN ENGLISH

Thursday began with the end of the month.

Rita came in at five-thirty through the back, the way she had come in for six years, and she did the day in its order. Lights. Coffee on. Stools down, last stool first. Pie case: two chess, one coconut, half a chocolate. The front door glass was bare this morning — the printed notice had come down at last, not because anything had changed back, but because a warning nobody needs anymore is just paper, and what it had promised was now simply the room: tables till six, counter after, the way the last weeks had run. There was a calendar on the wall by the register that the store side put up every year, with a truck on it, and this morning its month was full — every square crossed or lived in, down to the one she was standing in. The end of the month. She had known the date the way she knew everybody's dates, without being told twice, since a night in this kitchen when a man had set his coffee down and not picked it up again and told her the whole of a thing, all the way through, including the date on it.

At the pass she took down a plate and a square of wrap and cut Thursday's slice of chess and covered it and set it on the back shelf, and her knife did not stop over the pie. Whatever the date meant, it was not a date in her book. It was a date in a van, on a letter, in an office off a bypass in Jackson, and the day had not asked her to stop feeding people, and habit — which had won every hard week of this season — won this one while the room was still dark.

She put her name tag on over her heart and counted the drawer and opened the room, and the room, for all its dates, was still the room.

The Thursday cast came in on their clocks.

The produce man came in at a quarter past six with his cap cold from the lot, Thursday his double day, and took his counter stool the way he took it now, without rehearsal, a scholar settled into his studies for good. "End of the month," he said, over his eggs. "Road pays its bills and starts lying to itself again on the first. Man's no different."

"Man's no different," Rita said, and set his check where he could reach it, because on his double day he paid standing up.

The tanker man out of Memphis took the last stool without looking at it and turned his cup once in its saucer and said he had read no papers and signed nothing and intended to die in that condition, and Rita fed him like a man whose intentions were sound. Old Harlan came in at seven with his newspaper and one opinion already unfolded, and for once the opinion had narrowed to a point.

"Fellow at the fuel desk," Harlan announced, "says Jackson's made its pick. Says the corridor man's been chosen and there's an office with his name going on the door of it this week, and the signing starts out of that one hand, same as I told you." He folded his paper with the care of a man handling evidence. "Shape of it's come true, Rita. I hate it when that happens. Spoils a man's breakfast opinions, being right."

"A fellow at a fuel desk," Rita said, and refilled him without being asked, "ain't seen a signature any more than you have, Harlan. Eat your eggs while a name on a door is still a rumor and not a report."

"Eggs is best eaten ahead of all reports," Harlan said, and allowed the sports page to console him, and the morning went on — whole, and ordinary, and lit, with a date standing in it that nobody in the room said again.

At two, Marla came over from the store side with the deposit bag, the way she came over every day at two, and for the first time in a month of papers she had nothing in her other hand. "No paper for you today," she said, squaring the bag with the counter's edge the way she squared everything. "Office is quiet. Quietest it's been since the measuring. Whatever that means."
