PREVIEW — IN ENGLISH

Tuesday showed itself at the walk-in door.

Rita came in at five-thirty with her keys already in her hand, through the back, the way she had come in for six years, and started the room in its order, the way she always started it, so that if anything was wrong she would know it by the time she reached it. Lights. Coffee on. Stools down, last stool first. Pie case: two chess, one coconut, half a chocolate. The sign on the register said what it had said since last year — KITCHEN CLOSES 9:30 — and the photograph taped beside it, Merle's granddaughter in her pink hat, two months old and famous in this room, had curled at one corner again. Rita straightened the tape, counted the drawer, and put her name tag on over her heart. RITA. Hair pinned up, pencil behind one ear.

The walk-in was last on the round, the way it was always last, and this morning the wrongness was not a number. It was water.

Condensation stood in beads along the bottom edge of the door, and below the bottom corner a small dark puddle had gathered on the concrete, no bigger than a saucer. The steel at that corner was wet to the wrist when she wiped it. Rita knew that corner. It was the corner where a dollar bill had slid out easy on Thursday, when the closer was tightened to hold it a while longer, and where the check after had found the rubber split fine as a hair. A part had been ordered. Today was the day for it — a Tuesday, chosen the way these things were chosen, because Tuesday's lunch could be worked around a door standing open and Thursday's could not spare the hands.

She dried the corner with the bar rag and wrung the rag into the mop bucket and set a dry one folded at the threshold, to catch what gathered while the morning ran.

The dial over the door said thirty-six. Holding. She rested her palm a moment against the cold steel, above the wet corner, and then she went on, because the room was opening and the road was already coming in out of the lot.

The morning ran the way she knew it would run, and she ran it out of the book of her head without a ticket being written wrong or a cup set down in the wrong place.

The produce man took his booth and his coffee the way he always took them and wanted the big breakfast when his double run allowed it and the gravy over everything it could lawfully cover. Old Harlan came in at seven with his newspaper and his opinions and wanted his eggs drowned, the way he always wanted them, and toast to chase them with. The tanker man out of Memphis took the last stool without looking at it and wanted black coffee and quiet and got both. A woman traveling with two kids in a booth wanted the chess pie cut in three and a coffee she could drink while it was still hot, and Rita cut it in three without making her ask twice. A young driver with a reefer load wanted to know if he would make his drop, and Rita added it up out loud for him — his clock, his miles, the work at the river — and fed him eggs while the adding still held.

Every order in the room she knew before it finished being said. Most of them she knew before the saying started. That was the trade, and the room took it as weather, and by nine the rush had thinned to its bones and the folded rag at the walk-in threshold was dark with what the corner had wept, and Rita wrung it out again and folded it back down.

Ellis came in late in the morning, ahead of the lunch trade, through the kitchen door.

The door sighed on its closer and he set his toolbox down inside it, the way he had set it down for six years, in work clothes, cap in his back pocket. Under his other arm he carried the new gasket, coiled round as a rope in its bag, the rubber black and clean and still holding the flat folds where it had been packed for shipping.
