ABOUT THIS EPISODE

목요일 아침, 정문 유리에 인쇄된 공지가 붙는다 — 다음 달 1일부터 저녁 6시에 테이블 서비스가 끝나고, 이후 손님은 카운터에서 주문하고 카운터에서 먹는다. 그리고 봄의 동결 메모에 덴마크 주방이 폐기 검토 목록에 올라 있었다는 것, Ellis가 그걸 알고도 말하지 않았다는 것이 드러난다. 그날 밤 그는 “Thursday, then”이라고 말하지만, Rita는 처음으로 목요일을 돌려주지 않는다.

PREVIEW — IN ENGLISH

Thursday had a new sign on the front door.

Rita found it at six, when she went up front to unlock, with the room behind her already started in its order. Lights. Coffee on. Stools down, last stool first. Pie case: two chess, one coconut, half a chocolate. At the pass she had covered a slice of the chess and set it on the back shelf, the way Thursdays had taught her hands to do before the rest of her had caught up, and counted the drawer, and put her name tag on over her heart. RITA. Hair pinned up, pencil behind one ear. The hand-lettered sign on the register said what it had said since last year — KITCHEN CLOSES 9:30 — long enough that nobody read it anymore.

The new sign was printed, not lettered. Somebody from the evening side had taped it to the inside of the front door glass, straight and squared, at the height where a person coming in would meet it with his eyes before he met anything else in the room. Black type on white paper, under the company's name in the company's letters.

Rita stood on her side of the glass and read it twice.

The first time she read it for what it said. Effective the first of next month, the dining room would end table service at six in the evening. After 6 P.M., guests would order and pay at the counter. Tables would close at six. Thank you for your understanding, it said, in the tone paper uses when it is not asking.

The second time she read it for what it did not say. It did not say the kitchen closed earlier. The kitchen would still close at nine-thirty, the way it had closed since last year's hand-lettered sign went up and became a scar. The food would still be cooked. Men would still be fed. What ended at six was the room — the booths with their lights, a server coming to the table, a family settling in with the whole evening in front of them. After six there would be stools, and a counter, and a man carrying his own plate to it, and the tables standing dark behind him like furniture in a house nobody lived in anymore.

She unlocked the door and stood a moment with her hand on the bolt, looking at the paper from the wrong side, its words backward through the glass.

Last year's cut had come the same way. All the cuts came the same way. First men measured a room and ate nothing. Then the money for the room's heart stopped, quiet as a held breath. Then a piece of paper went up where people could read it, and the paper had a date on it, and the date came. Measuring was weather. A date was a date.

"Well," Rita said, to the empty room, and went back to the counter, because Thursday does not wait for a woman to finish reading.

Thursdays had their own cast, and the cast read the sign one at a time, at the register, with coffee in front of them.

The produce man came in at a quarter past six with his cap still cold from the lot. Thursday was his double day — Memphis in the morning empty and back through by dark full — and he read the door over her shoulder while she poured.

"Counter after six," he said. "What's that when it's at home?"

"It means at six the tables close," Rita said. "You want supper after six, you order it at the counter and pay at the counter and eat it at the counter. Stools stay. Booths go dark."

"So a man eats his supper the way he eats his fuel," he said. "Standing at the pump." He considered it the way he considered weather on his route, without surprise. "First of next month, that's my supper you're describing."
