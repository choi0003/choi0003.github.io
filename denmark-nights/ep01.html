Episode 1: Through the Kitchen Door

DENMARK NIGHTS — VOLUME 1: THE FRONT DOOR

덴마크 나이츠 1편 · 영어 원문
Denmark Nights, Episode 1 of 14

글 최도미 (Choi Domi)

Denmark Nights 제1편입니다. 이 편은 영어 원문 전문을 싣습니다. (전문 공개는 1편과 2편까지이고, 3편부터는 제목과 한 줄 소개만 열립니다.)

Rita came in at five-thirty, the way she had come in for six years, through the back, with her keys already in her hand.

The lot was full and quiet. Trucks sat in their rows with their curtains drawn, a few idling, the reefer two rows over cycling on and off the way it had all night. The sky over Providence Road was going the color of dishwater brightening. She let herself in the staff door, punched her code, and started the room the way she always started it, in the same order, so that if anything was wrong she would know it by the time she reached it.

Lights. Coffee on. Stools down off the counter, in reverse — last stool first, the way she put them up at night. Pie case checked: two chess, one coconut, half a chocolate. Register counted. The hand-lettered sign still taped where it had been taped since last year — KITCHEN CLOSES 9:30 — long enough that nobody read it anymore, the way nobody reads a scar. On the side of the register, a photograph: a baby in a pink hat, Merle's granddaughter, two months old and already famous in this room. Rita straightened the tape on it out of habit.

Her name tag went on last, over her heart, the way it had gone on for six years. RITA. Somewhere past fifty, hair pinned up, pencil behind one ear. She could have set the room up in the dark. Some mornings she nearly did.

The dining room woke up in her head before it woke up in fact, because Rita carried it there — who was due through and when, who took the corner booth and who took a stool, whose coffee was black and whose was half sugar, whose clock had an hour on it and whose had twenty minutes and needed to eat like it. The produce man would want the booth and the gravy. The tanker driver out of Memphis would want the last stool and wouldn't say ten words. Old Harlan would come in at seven with his newspaper and his opinions and want his eggs drowned. She knew the whole morning the way a dispatcher knows a board, and the morning, most days, did what she knew it would do.

She had noticed it at the door, before the lights, and put it aside the way you put aside a wrong note in a song you know by heart: the building sounded different this morning. A building has a sound. Coolers and cases and the ice machine, all of them humming their separate hums in one chord, and Rita knew the chord the way she knew her own street. This morning, one note of it sat a quarter-tone flat. She would hear it again at the end of her round, where she had saved the heart for last.

The walk-in was last on the round, the way it was always last, because the walk-in was the kitchen's heart and you saved the heart for when you could pay attention.

The dial over the door said forty-seven.

It was supposed to say thirty-six.

Rita stood still with her hand on the door handle and felt the wrongness come up through the steel before she opened it — cool where it should have been cold. She pulled it open. The air that came out was the air of a refrigerator left standing with its door cracked. The milk crates were sweating. A tray of cut melon shone wet. The butter was going soft at the edges of the blocks.

She did not swear. She started moving.

Eggs, milk, opened cream, the sliced cheese, the raw chicken from the bottom shelf — into the reach-in under the pass, stacked fast and flat, the way you'd move furniture out of a rising creek. The things too big to move she pushed to the back of the box, against the coldest wall. Eleven minutes. She had done breakdowns before. A walk-in doesn't give you its trouble all at once; it gives you a warm morning and a clock, and the clock on spoiled food in this state was health-department arithmetic that Rita knew by heart: above forty-one degrees, the count was running.

The breakfast cook came in at five-fifty in the middle of it, took one look at the open walk-in and the stacked reach-in, and started moving pans without being told anything except the number.

"Forty-seven," Rita said.

"Then it's eggs and the reach-in till the man comes," the cook said, "and nobody in this room orders a salad before noon anyway." They worked the way people work who have worked a bad morning together before. There would be a list, later, of what had to be thrown out if the box didn't come back down by ten. Neither of them said the word list.

Then she picked up the phone by the register and dialed the number on the card taped beside it, which she knew by heart and read anyway, the way careful people do. MID-SOUTH REFRIGERATION — JACKSON. NIGHT SERVICE.

The night desk answered on the third ring. A man's voice, awake the way night people are awake.

"Mid-South."

"This is Rita at the Denmark stop. The walk-in's warm. Forty-seven and it was working all night to get there."

A pause with keys in it. "Brandt's just clearing a freezer call on the bypass. I'll put him to you soon as he's rolling. Thirty minutes, give or take."

"Tell him it's the walk-in," Rita said.

"Tell him yourself. He'll want the particulars." The man said it without any meanness. It was just how it was known to work. You didn't describe a box to Ellis Brandt secondhand, any more than you'd describe weather.

She opened the dining room at six with what she had. Coffee out. The first drivers came in out of the lot in their shower sandals and their tiredness, read the short menu she called from the pass — eggs she could do, anything off the reach-in she could do — and took it the way the road takes everything, as information. Word went through the room faster than she could have carried it.

"Cooler man's on his way," she told the produce man, and set his coffee down, and he nodded like she'd given him a forecast.

At twenty past six the kitchen door sighed on its closer — a sound Rita could have picked out of any noise in the building — and Ellis Brandt set his toolbox down inside it.

He was sixty-one years old and he came in the way he had come in for six years: through the back, in work clothes, cap in his back pocket, his name stitched over his shirt pocket in case anybody needed it, which in six years nobody in this building ever had. He had been up since two, on a store freezer over in Jackson, and it showed in him only in the way he moved — a man spending himself carefully, like the last of the cash.

"Morning," he said.

"It was," Rita said.

He didn't go to the walk-in. He stood just inside the kitchen door with his bag at his feet and listened — to the whole building, the compressors and the reach-in and the little ice machine, the way a doctor listens at a door before he knocks. Rita had watched him do it for six years and it still settled a room, that listening. It said: nothing in here is going to get past me.

"There," he said at last, more to himself than to her. "Hear that?"

She heard the walk-in's condensing fan come on somewhere out back, and in its note a wobble, a roughness, like a wheel with a flat spot.

"I hear a wobble," she said.

"You hear a bearing going." He picked up the bag. "Let's go see what she's wearing."

The condensing unit sat on a concrete pad behind the kitchen, in a fenced corner with the grease trap and the mop buckets, and it was wearing a sweater. The coil was matted blind with dust and cottonwood fluff and the fine gray lint of ten thousand trucks. Ellis got down beside it on one knee — down slow, a hand on the unit, the knee announcing itself — and Rita, who had followed him out with his coffee without either of them deciding she would, set the cup on the pad within his reach and stood in the kitchen doorway, which was her side of the threshold.

"Coil can't breathe," he said. "And that fan motor's got the wobble. Bearing's done." He laid the back of his hand near the discharge line the way you'd feel a forehead. "She's working three times as hard to do half the cooling. That's your forty-seven."

"What about the compressor?"

She knew the vocabulary. Six years of his invoices had taught her the difference between the fan and the heart, the way six years of her counter had taught him not to ask for a menu.

"The compressor's tired, Rita." He said it plain, working while he said it, hose washing the coil out in gray sheets. "It's not quitting today. It's not quitting this month, most likely. It's losing a step at a time. It cools. It just cools slower than the day heats. You feel it on the warm mornings first."

He swapped the fan motor for the one off his van — "This box has eaten two of these in six years. I carry its size." From the kitchen doorway Rita could see past the parts shelves into the van's cab: a blanket roll, a thermos, a pair of boots set neat on the floor mat. The cab of a man who slept in it in pieces, between calls, in lots like this one. She had never asked where Ellis Brandt lived. Six years, and everything she knew about his home fit in the back of that van.

"Last one of those motors was three hundred and eighty, part and labor," she said. "Two years ago, March."

Ellis didn't look up from the wrench. "Three ninety-five now. Everything's up but the pay." He turned the last bolt home with his stiff ring finger riding useless along the wrench, and snugged a seeping fitting that had been weeping refrigerant in a faint oily breath, and put his gauges on and read them the way other men read a letter. The wobble went out of the note. The unit settled into a lower, even hum, like a dog lying down.

Then, standing at the pad with the box pulling itself back toward cold, he took the small book out of his chest pocket and photographed the serial plate on the unit with his phone, and wrote something in the book in a ruled column, a number, and closed it.

"What's the number mean?" Rita asked, though she had told herself she wouldn't.

"Four. On a scale where five's a box they'd bury." He put the book away. "Four's a box they nurse. I grade 'em honest, Rita. It's the only line on that paper that's mine."

She did not ask what the paper was for. There were piles of paper in this business that were none of hers, and a woman who worked a counter learned which piles were hers by the time she'd worked it six years.

"Coffee's getting cold," she said.

He drank it standing on the pad, in the brightening morning, with the trucks idling beyond the fence. "What's the book say I'll owe?" she asked, because the room would ask her.

"Motor and the call. Compressor's the conversation." He wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist. "I put the recommendation in back in the spring. In writing. New compressor, sized right, before this one goes from tired to done. The answer's the same as everybody's answer since spring." He said it without bitterness, the way you report rain. "The company's froze the equipment money. All of it, all the old kitchens on the corridor. My paper's in a pile with everybody else's paper."

"The men who measured the room last fall," Rita said. "That pile."

"That pile."

She thought about the pile. She thought about the dining room going the way of the nine-thirty sign, one posted notice at a time, and about the store in Jackson that had been measured two years before this one and was still standing, because measured never yet meant gone.

"So what do we do with a tired compressor and no money?"

"We nurse it," Ellis said. "A box like this, you don't fix it once. You keep it company. I come by Thursday nights, on my route — check the pressures, clean what's dirty, listen to her. Catch the drift early and a tired compressor will run a season. Maybe two. Summer's the test." He capped his pen. "She'll tell me when she's done. She won't surprise us. Boxes don't surprise you if you're listening."

Thursday, Rita thought. She kept everybody's days — Harlan's long Thursdays, the tanker man's runs, Dee's shifts, Merle's nights — in the book of her head, and now Ellis Brandt's Thursdays went into it, in the column where she kept everything. She did not examine why the column felt different. A counter woman doesn't examine. She pours.

"Thursday nights, then," she said. "Back light'll be on."

"Back light's always on, Rita."

"Now it has a reason."

He almost smiled at that. Six years, and the almost-smile at the kitchen door was the whole of what there was, and it was not nothing. It was the shape of the place.

The breakfast rush came in on top of the repair the way weather comes in on top of a roof being mended, without caring. Rita went back to the counter and the room stood up around her. Coffee landed in front of the produce man before he'd finished sitting down, and his order went back to the kitchen in the same breath as his hello. Old Harlan got his eggs drowned and his opinion in first. The tanker man out of Memphis took the last stool without looking at it, the way he always took it, and got his black coffee and his ten words' worth of quiet, and Rita let him have both. A young driver, eleven hours out and hollow-eyed, asked what was good and Rita told him, "Eggs. The box had a morning," and fed him without making him decide anything else. A driver with a reefer load asked her whether he'd make his drop, and she added it up out loud for him — his clock, his miles, the construction at the river — and said, "You've got time for eggs if the eggs are quick." He ate like a man who'd been given permission, which in a way he had. This was her trade and she was its master and the room knew it, and there was a plain pleasure in it that she would not have traded for a quieter life even on the mornings she was tired. She was tired. She had been tired since five-thirty. The room didn't know, because the room was not supposed to know, because knowing things was her job and being known was nobody's.

At the height of it a driver heading out paused at the register with his hat in his hands. "You see Danny through here Friday, you tell him the Jackson load's covered. Tell him not to deadhead home empty on my account."

"I'll tell him," Rita said.

In the first breath after the rush thinned she took the worn notebook from behind the register — its cover gone soft as cloth, a rubber band around it twice — and wrote it in, in her small square hand, among the ride requests and the phone numbers and the word left for men who were three states away: Danny — Jackson load covered, don't deadhead. Fri. The book was where the road left word for itself, because the road had learned that Rita was where word kept.

At the counter, two drivers were doing what drivers do with news, which is pass it without owning it.

"You see the flags at the next exit?"

"Stakes. Orange ones, all along the fence line. And a frame up for a billboard."

"They say a travel plaza. Two hundred spots."

"They've had stakes in that field since spring. A billboard ain't a building."

"It's the start of one."

Rita refilled their cups and said nothing. News arrived at that counter the way weather arrived, late and partial, and she had long since learned not to forecast.

Ellis ate at nine, when the box had pulled down to thirty-eight and holding. Rita set the plate at the pass without a word exchanged about it — eggs scrambled soft, bacon crisp, toast already buttered, coffee black — the way she had set it, some weeks, for six years, and he ate it standing on the kitchen side of the pass with his toolbox at his feet, the way he had eaten every plate she had ever handed him. Standing up. On his way through. Her own breakfast had been the end of a toast rack, eaten over the sink between orders, the way it always was. If anyone in the building had ever asked Rita what she ate, she'd have had to think about it, and so would they.

"You been seeing the man long?" the produce man said to her at the register, meaning nothing by it, paying his ticket, nodding toward the kitchen. "Six years I been coming through here. His van, anyway. Never once seen the man himself come in the front door."

"He comes the way he comes," Rita said.

She wrote up nothing and decided nothing. When the box's hum had steadied and the ticket was torn — his copy on top, in his square capitals, COND and a number she didn't read — she took her copy and slid it into the notebook behind the register, in among the road's messages, where she kept all of his. Six years of them. Then she wrote Thurs. in her small hand under the corner of it, to remember to leave the back light on, as if she would have forgotten.

Ellis picked up his bag. The kitchen door sighed.

"Thursday, then," he said.

"Thursday," she said. "See you when I see you."

"See you when I see you, Rita."

The door closed behind him the way it had closed behind him six hundred times, on its sighing closer, and a moment later the front door chimed for a stranger — a driver she'd never seen, shaking the road out of his shoulders, looking for a stool. The front door chimed for everybody in the world, some mornings.

It had never once chimed for Ellis Brandt.

At ten the cook wrote the list — one tray of melon and the butter nobody could have saved — and read it aloud like a score. It could have been a truckload. They had both seen a truckload.

At noon she checked the dial over the walk-in door on her way past with a stack of menus. Thirty-seven. Holding. She stood a moment with her palm flat against the cold steel, the way you'd rest a hand on a shoulder, and then she went on.

Behind the kitchen wall the walk-in hummed, lower now, even — the sound of a thing being kept company. Rita picked up the coffee pot and went back to work.
