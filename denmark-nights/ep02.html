Episode 2: Thursday, Then

DENMARK NIGHTS — VOLUME 1: THE FRONT DOOR

덴마크 나이츠 2편 · 영어 원문
Denmark Nights, Episode 2 of 14

글 최도미 (Choi Domi)

Denmark Nights 제2편입니다. 이 편은 영어 원문 전문을 싣습니다. (전문 공개는 1편과 2편까지이고, 3편부터는 소개와 미리보기만 열립니다.)

A week went by the way weeks go at a truck stop, which is one truck at a time, and then it was Thursday.

Rita came in at five-thirty with her keys already in her hand, and before the lights, before the coffee, she stepped out the back door onto the pad and looked up at the light over the kitchen door. It burned the way it burned every night of the year. She looked at it the whole time it took her to find the right key, which was longer than finding the key took.

Lights. Coffee on. Stools down off the counter, in reverse. Pie case: two chess, one coconut, half a chocolate — the same census as last Thursday, because Thursdays started from the same place. She cut a slice of the chess, a clean slice, and set it on a plate and covered it with wrap and put it on the back shelf of the pass, where things waited that were not on any ticket.

The breakfast cook came in at five-fifty and saw the plate while he was tying his apron.

"That one spoken for?"

"It'll keep," Rita said.

On the side of the register the parts-house calendar hung with the month squared off in her small hand — deliveries, Dee's payday, the produce man's double day. Thursday's square was empty. Her pencil stopped over it a moment while she counted the drawer, the way a song stops on a note it already knows, and then she went on counting and wrote nothing in it.

Her name tag went on last, over her heart. RITA. Hair pinned up, pencil behind one ear. She could have set the room up in the dark. This morning she had done part of it that way on purpose and would have denied it under oath.

Thursdays had their own cast, the way every day at the stop had its own cast and its own weather, and Rita held the whole week of them in her head the way she held the orders — without writing it down, because writing it down was for things that might be forgotten, and she had not forgotten a Thursday yet.

The produce man came in at six-fifteen with his cap still cold from the lot. Thursday was his double day — Memphis in the morning with an empty trailer and back through by dark with a full one, two loads of road in one shirt — and he ate on Thursdays the way a man fuels, twice and without ceremony.

"Thursday," he said, sitting down, the way other men said a bad word.

"Thursday," Rita said, and put the big breakfast in front of him before he'd finished deciding to order it, and the coffee black, and the check where he could reach it without asking, because on his double day he paid standing up on his way out and hated to wait for arithmetic.

The tanker man out of Memphis took the last stool without looking at it. Thursday was his far point. He turned around tonight, slept in his own bed tomorrow, and the whole of his week bent around the turning. She knew it the way she knew the weather of him, and she fed him like a man with a long way still in front of him today and a short way home at the end of it — eggs quick, coffee often, no conversation charged against the clock.

"You turn tonight," she said.

"I turn tonight," he said, and that was the ten words spent well.

At seven Old Harlan came in with his newspaper and his opinions and wanted his eggs drowned. Thursday was Harlan's long day at the counter — nowhere on earth he had to be, and a man with nowhere to be will make a morning last — and Rita let the opinions come in the order they came, weather and tomatoes and the county, and refilled him without being asked, because on Thursdays Harlan's coffee going cold was a kind of failure she had decided long ago not to allow.

A woman driver came through on Thursdays only, hauling flowers out of the greenhouses to Memphis, and on Thursdays she ate pie for breakfast at the counter with her coffee, unashamed, the road owing her one sweet thing a week and her collecting it. Rita had the chess plate down in front of her before her keys stopped swinging.

"One of these weeks I'll have the eggs like a citizen," the woman said.

"One of these weeks," Rita said.

In the first lull, a company man in a clean shirt stopped at the register on his way out with his hat in his hand. "You see Okafor through here, you tell him his spare keys turned up. Jackson fuel desk, under his name. Tell him he don't have to hot-wire his own truck no more."

"I'll tell him," Rita said.

She took the worn notebook from behind the register — cover gone soft as cloth, rubber band around it twice — and wrote it in her small square hand among the ride requests and the phone numbers and the word left for men three states away: Okafor — spare keys, Jackson fuel desk, under your name. The book was where the road left word for itself, because the road had learned that Rita was where word kept.

Dee came on at two with her school envelope in her apron, the way she always came on, tips going into the envelope before anything else went anywhere. On the schedule board by the office door, the Thursday close had Rita's name in Rita's hand, over a penciled Dee, erased but showing through.

"You took my close," Dee said.

"You got Thursday nights back," Rita said.

"I didn't ask for Thursday nights back."

"A body's got to have one night that's theirs," Rita said, and went to refill the sugar pourers, and that was the whole of the discussion there was ever going to be.

In the middle of the afternoon, when the room was down to two drivers and the long light, one of them paid at the register and said, to nobody in particular, "Survey buggy was back out at the field this morning. Fella walking it with one of them measuring wheels."

"That so," Rita said, and refilled his cup for the road, and the talk moved on the way talk at that counter moved on, to the river construction and the price of showers.

The produce man came back through at seven with the full trailer, the second half of his Thursday completing itself the way it always completed itself, and he ate his supper standing at the register end of the counter because sitting down would have finished him for the night. Rita had his ticket already written when he came through the door. He ate looking at nothing, paid standing up, and went out into the dark with his double day done, and the room was quieter for a circuit having closed.

The kitchen closed at nine-thirty, the way it had closed since the hand-lettered sign went up last year, and the dining room thinned to its last men. Merle hung up his apron and checked his phone — Thursday nights his daughter sent the baby's pictures, and the whole kitchen knew to give him the ten minutes it took — and he wiped his station down slow and went home to them.

"Box man coming tonight?" he said at the door.

"Thursday," Rita said.

"Thursday," Merle said, and went.

At twenty to ten the kitchen door sighed on its closer, in the quiet room, and Ellis Brandt set his toolbox down inside it.

He was sixty-one years old and he came in the way he had come in for six years: through the back, in work clothes, cap in his back pocket. But it was not a call this time, and nothing was wrong, and the room was empty and cooling toward close, and he stood just inside the door with his bag at his feet and listened to the building the way he had listened the week before — except that a building at night, with the dining room dark at the edges and the store humming through the wall, had a different chord, and he stood in it a moment the way a man stands in a familiar doorway.

"Thursday," he said.

"Thursday," she said.

He went to the walk-in first. The dial over the door said thirty-eight.

"Holding," she said.

"Warm day to hold it, too." He read the number the way he read everything about that box, without hurry.

He opened the box and stepped inside a moment, into the cold, and stood among the crates listening to the fans over his head the way he listened to everything. Then he bent and pulled two milk crates an inch off the back wall, and a case of eggs after them.

"She breathes through there," he said. "Air's got to make the whole trip around. Stack her tight to the wall and she cools the front and starves the back."

"I stack them to the wall every morning of the world," Rita said.

"I know it," he said. "I move them every call I make here. Six years we've been having this arrangement."

"Let's go listen to her outside, then," she said.

Out back, the night was full of the lot — trucks idling in rows, a reefer cycling, somebody's radio low. Ellis brushed the condenser coil down with a soft brush, working the lint off the fin face in gray sheets, and pulled the matted edge of it free with his fingers. He ran a dollar bill around the walk-in's door gasket, slow, feeling for the drag — it held at the top and the sides, and at the bottom corner the bill slid loose as a tongue. He tightened the door closer two turns with a wrench and ran the bill again, and it held.

"Gasket's got a season in it yet," he said. "Door was just tired of closing."

He put his gauges on and read them the way other men read a letter, and stood a while with his hand near the discharge line, listening to the note of the thing under the note.

"Fifty-eight on the low side. Two-forty-one on the high."

"That good or bad?" she said.

"For a night this warm, she ought to run two-thirty-five on the high side to do this work. She's working a hair harder than last week to do the same cooling." He took the gauges off and coiled the hoses the way he coiled them, in order. "That's the drift. A hair at a time. It ain't trouble. It's her telling us the truth early, is all. Long as somebody's here Thursdays to hear it."

Then he took the small book out of his chest pocket and opened it flat on the pass to write, and Rita saw it properly for the first time — ruled columns, page after page of them, every row the same shape: a date, two numbers side by side, and a grade at the end of the row. Rows going back further than this spring. Further than she could see from where she stood, and she did not lean.

He wrote the date at the end of the last row. He wrote 58 and 241 in their columns. And in the last column, in the same square hand, he wrote 4.

"Still a four," she said.

"Still a four. Four's holding." He closed the book and put it away in his chest pocket and capped his pen, and that was all there was to it, the way it had been all there was to it last week — a man, a box, a number, and the paper going wherever the paper went, which was none of the piles that were hers.

His work was done. The box was checked, the coil was clean, the numbers were written. He stood a moment with his bag still on the floor where he'd set it down, and looked at the room — the stools still down, the pie case lit, the close still ahead of her — and instead of picking the bag up he said, "Stools?"

"Stools," she said.

She started at her end of the counter, last stool first, the way she always started. He took the far end. He lifted them the way she lifted them — set up square on the counter, not swung, not dropped — and what she noticed, standing in her own rhythm with the room going quiet around the work, was that his end kept time with hers. When she stopped to straighten the menu stack by the register, the lifting from the far end stopped too, and started again when she started, and neither of them said a word about the tempo of it, the way musicians don't.

They met in the middle, at the register, where the stools ran out. His eyes went to the photograph taped to the register's side — a baby in a pink hat, two months old and already famous in that room.

"That the cook's?" he said.

"Granddaughter," Rita said. "Merle's girl's baby. He cooks all night for the whole interstate and goes home and practices on a person weighs nine pounds."

"He's done for, then," Ellis said.

"He was done for at the first picture," Rita said.

The pie case was last. She took the inside of the glass with one cloth and he took the outside with the second cloth, the two of them moving along the case the way they'd moved along the counter, a man cleaning glass that was not his glass in a room that was not his room, past the hour his errand ended.

Then she set the morning's plate at the pass — the covered slice of chess, where it had waited all day on the back shelf — and took the wrap off it without ceremony.

"There's chess pie, if you want it standing up," she said.

He ate it standing at the pass with his toolbox at his feet, the way he had eaten every plate she had ever handed him, and the coffee she poured went beside it without either of them arranging that either.

"Chess on a Thursday, then," he said.

"It's the pie there was," she said.

He carried his plate and cup to the kitchen sink and rinsed them and set them in the rack, the way he left every place he worked — nothing of him behind but the work itself. In six years, no other man who had ever eaten in that kitchen had washed his own plate. Rita had noticed it in the first year and filed it where she filed everything, and it had stayed filed, and it came up now the way filed things do when a room goes empty.

He picked up his bag at last. The kitchen door waited on its closer.

"See you when I see you," she said.

"See you when I see you, Rita."

The door sighed shut behind him. She finished the close the way she finished every close — drawer counted, lights banked, the room set to rights for a morning she would open herself — and took the trash out the back last of all, the bag tied, the pad dark under the light that burned the way it burned every night of the year.

His van was still there.

It sat at the far edge of the lot by the fence, dark, engine off, where he always parked it out of the trucks' way — and in the cab, in the spill of the lot lights, the shape of a man sitting, not sleeping that she could tell, just sitting, with nowhere in the next minute he had to be. She put the bag in the dumpster and came back in and did not look a second time. Some things a counter woman filed the way she filed the days of the week, in the book of her head, in the column where Thursdays went, and let them keep.

In the notebook behind the register, behind last week's copy with its COND in his square capitals, she slid tonight's — the tear slip from the check, WKLY CK and the date and the two numbers — the second one that had ever come on a Thursday with nothing wrong. Six years of his copies she had kept, and now two of them were Thursdays. She wrote Thurs. beside it in her small square hand, underneath the corner, the same word in the same place, though she knew it by heart and would have remembered — she wrote the things that mattered in that book in case memory ever needed help.

It never had.

Up front, the door stood locked and dark, its chime off for the night. It had never once chimed for Ellis Brandt.
