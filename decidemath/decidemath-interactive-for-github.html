Key Ideas

The handful of ideas this whole chapter stands on. If one of these feels shaky, the practice above will feel shaky too.

A variable is a placeholder with a job

A variable is a letter that stands for a quantity that can change or that you don't know yet. Priya doesn't write "number of cups sold" forty times — she writes n and lets n be whatever Saturday brings. One letter, every possible Saturday.

Expressions describe; equations assert

An algebraic expression combines variables, numbers, and operations — 3x + 5, 2(n − 4), 11.50h + 1.25b — and has no equals sign. It describes a quantity. An equation (Chapter 2) asserts that two quantities are equal.

Translation trap: "less than" flips

"5 less than a number" is n − 5, not 5 − n. "Less than" starts from the number and takes away. Test it with n = 20: 20 − 5 = 15 makes sense; 5 − 20 = −15 does not. The same care applies to "8 years younger than you": n − 8.

Parts of an expression

In Marcus's pay, 25l + 10h + 12: the terms are 25l, 10h, and 12. A coefficient is the number multiplying a variable — a rate, like $25 per lawn. A constant never changes: the flat $12. Like terms share the same variable and power — 25l + 25l combine into 50l; 25l and 10h never combine.

Evaluating = substitute, then compute

To evaluate an expression, replace each variable with a number and compute. Devon's bill is 30 + 8g; at g = 3 extra gigabytes, 30 + 8(3) = 30 + 24 = $54. The expression is the machine; substitution feeds it.

Order of operations — and why parentheses rule

Parentheses first, then exponents, then multiply/divide left to right, then add/subtract left to right. Here's the question to sit with: a $48 dinner, a $10 coupon, and a 15% tip. Your friend says tip on the full $48, then subtract the coupon. You say coupon first, then tip on what's left. Work out both totals yourself — do they match? Which amount is the 15% actually tipping on in each method? Most restaurants tip on the discounted total.

Essay Studio — The Decision Essay

The prompt. Your cousin got the same two summer job offers from §1.1: Build-a-Box Warehouse ($11.50/hour + $1.25 per box packed) and City Scoops ($14.00/hour flat). She will work 20 hours a week but has no idea how many boxes she'll pack — it could be 30 or 300. Write a 3-paragraph essay: which job should she take? Defend your recommendation with this chapter's math — and be honest about when your advice would change. Length: 200–300 words, exactly three paragraphs.

Step 1 — Compute first

Write both pay expressions (h = hours/week, b = boxes/week), evaluate them at two box counts, and find the breakeven. Enter your numbers below — the studio checks each one as Match or No match yet and never shows you the answers. If one doesn't match, recheck your arithmetic and try again.

Step 2 — Thesis template

Paragraph 1 needs one clear recommendation. Fill in this frame in your own words, then use it as your opening sentence:

"My cousin should take the ____________ job, because the math shows that ____________."

One strong ending names your breakeven: "…because the breakeven point is only ______ boxes a week" — your number, from your own computation in Step 1.

Step 3 — Evidence checklist

Paragraph 2 is only as strong as its numbers. Before you draft it, confirm you have all four ready:

Both pay expressions, written correctly.
Two evaluations at different box counts, with the paychecks side by side.
The breakeven point, in boxes per week.
One sentence saying what the breakeven means: above it one job wins, below it the other wins.

Step 4 — The flip test

Paragraph 3 must name one specific condition that would flip your recommendation. Write it here first:

"My advice would change if ____________, because then ____________."

Possible flips to consider: she packs fewer boxes than the breakeven each week; the warehouse caps the box bonus; her hours drop below 20 a week. Also name what your math doesn't capture at all — how tiring the work is, or which job she would simply enjoy.

Draft your three paragraphs

Write your first draft entirely on your own. Paragraph 1: your decision and claim. Paragraph 2: the mathematical evidence. Paragraph 3: implications, limitations, and what would change your mind.

Total: 0 words — target is 200–300 words.
